Rank AI opportunities without chasing every pain point

Turn observed work friction into a transparent shortlist using value, feasibility, risk, and readiness.

Reviewed 2026-08-01 · review again by 2026-11-01

What this helps decide

Use this guide to decide which observed opportunity deserves a closer experiment. Ranking makes judgment visible; it does not turn uncertain estimates into guaranteed returns.

Before you start

  • Bring a short list of evidence-backed pain points from actual work.
  • Name the decision owner and the people affected by each possible change.
  • Agree that a useful answer may be no AI project yet.
  • Use a simple low, medium, or high scale before reaching for invented precision.

The procedure

  1. Rewrite every candidate as a problem and a desired work outcome, not a preferred tool.
  2. Score value by the importance, frequency, and reach of the friction. Do not convert guesses into dollar claims.
  3. Score feasibility by access to the workflow, data suitability, technical complexity, and the team's ability to operate the result.
  4. Score risk across privacy, security, legal, reliability, human impact, and the consequence of a wrong answer.
  5. Score readiness by sponsor ownership, participant time, baseline evidence, and willingness to change the work.
  6. Write one sentence explaining each score. The explanation matters more than arithmetic.
  7. Remove candidates that require unresolved sensitive data, irreversible integrations, or authority the team does not have.
  8. Select at most one candidate for a bounded experiment and record why the others wait.

Evidence standard

Every score should point to an observation, constraint, named owner, or clearly labeled assumption. A high-value score without a recent example is a hypothesis. A low-risk score without reviewing the data and failure consequence is incomplete.

Common failure modes

  • Ranking ideas before agreeing on the problem.
  • Letting a compelling demo substitute for workflow evidence.
  • Hiding disagreement inside an averaged score.
  • Treating feasibility as only a coding question.
  • Choosing the largest possible project because it sounds strategic.

DIY fit

DIY ranking works when candidates belong to one workflow, the same small group can compare them, scores remain directional, and the decision is easy to revisit as evidence changes.

When to bring in facilitation

Bring in facilitation when competing leaders own different parts of the work, risk tolerance is disputed, the shortlist mixes several departments, data access is uncertain, or the ranking will be treated as an investment commitment rather than a learning decision.
