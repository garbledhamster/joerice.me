Design a bounded 90-day AI experiment

Define a reversible learning cycle with an owner, baseline, guardrails, checkpoints, and an explicit stop decision.

Reviewed 2026-08-01 · review again by 2026-11-01

  1. Frame

What this helps decide

Use this guide to decide whether a small change is worth continuing, revising, or stopping after a defined learning period. Ninety days is an outer boundary, not a promise that every experiment needs the full period.

Before you start

  • Select one problem and one team from the ranked opportunity list.
  • Name one accountable owner and a sponsor who can remove blockers.
  • Record the current way of working before changing it.
  • Identify the people who can stop the experiment if harm, data exposure, or operational instability appears.

The procedure

  1. Write one learning question. Example structure: can this bounded change help this role complete this step with acceptable quality and risk?
  2. Define the smallest change that can answer the question. Prefer a reversible manual or local prototype before an integration.
  3. Record the baseline using observable workflow evidence such as completion time bands, rework counts, error categories, or participant notes. Do not promise improvement.
  4. Set success, revise, and stop signals before the experiment begins.
  5. Define allowed inputs, prohibited inputs, required human review, access boundaries, and how experiment material will be removed or retained.
  6. Assign weekly ownership and schedule checkpoints around days 30, 60, and 90.
  7. At each checkpoint, compare evidence with the original question and record new risks or changed assumptions.
  8. End with one explicit decision: stop, revise and repeat, keep as a small team system, or separately evaluate a larger implementation.

Evidence standard

Keep the baseline, experiment record, and final decision together. Record exceptions and negative evidence, not only successful examples. Participant impressions can inform the decision but should not be presented as measured business outcomes.

Common failure modes

  • Calling an open-ended rollout an experiment.
  • Changing several workflow steps at once.
  • Choosing measures after seeing the results.
  • Testing with data or access that the team is not authorized to use.
  • Continuing because effort has already been spent.

DIY fit

DIY experimentation fits a small, reversible workflow change with a clear owner, low-consequence failure, approved inputs, and a sponsor willing to stop when the evidence says stop.

When to bring in facilitation

Bring in facilitation when the experiment crosses systems or departments, affects consequential decisions, uses sensitive data, lacks a neutral owner, needs specialist security or legal review, or is already being treated as a production implementation.
